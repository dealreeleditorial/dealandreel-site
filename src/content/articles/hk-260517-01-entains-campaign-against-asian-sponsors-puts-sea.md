# Entain's Campaign Against Asian Sponsors Puts SEA Operators on Notice

Entain has launched a legal and lobbying campaign targeting three Asian-facing bookmakers — BJ88, SBOTOP, and W88 — that hold Premier League shirt and stadium sponsorships without UK Gambling Commission (UKGC) licences. The move, running in parallel with a [UK Department for Culture, Media and Sport (DCMS) consultation on gambling advertising reform](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/consultation-on-gambling-advertising), threatens to disrupt a brand strategy that operators across Southeast Asia have used for years to project credibility in home markets.

## A Regulatory Gap Entain Wants Closed

The mechanism at the centre of Entain's campaign is a jurisdictional anomaly. UK law does not prohibit a foreign company from buying a Premier League sponsorship. What it prohibits is advertising gambling services to UK consumers without a UKGC licence. BJ88, SBOTOP, and W88 are not licensed by the UKGC, yet their logos appear on kits and stadium hoardings broadcast to millions of UK viewers each matchday.

The operators' position rests on a distinction between brand advertising — permissible for unlicensed entities — and direct promotion of gambling services to UK residents, which is not. That line has never been formally tested in the context of shirt sponsorships, and Entain appears to be betting that a sustained legal and lobbying effort can force either a regulatory clarification or a DCMS rule change that would effectively bar unlicensed operators from Premier League commercial deals.

The timing is not accidental. The DCMS consultation on gambling advertising is live, and the window for shaping its conclusions is narrow. Entain's intervention is designed to land while policymakers are already primed to tighten the rules. The [UKGC's guidance on remote gambling licences](https://www.gamblingcommission.gov.uk/licensees-and-businesses/guide/remote-gambling-and-software-technical-standards) sets out the statutory basis for what constitutes regulated advertising — a definition that may yet be applied to sponsorship hoardings.

## Competitive Logic, Dressed as Compliance

It would be a mistake to read Entain's campaign as purely altruistic regulatory advocacy. The company competes directly with Asian-facing operators for share of wallet in markets where Premier League football commands enormous cultural weight. A UKGC-licensed operator that spends heavily on compliance and responsible-gambling obligations has an obvious commercial interest in ensuring that unlicensed competitors cannot access the same brand-building channel at lower cost.

That does not make the underlying regulatory concern invalid. The UKGC has historically been alert to what it calls "grey market" exposure — situations where operators without UK licences nonetheless achieve significant brand recognition among UK consumers. Whether shirt sponsorships constitute advertising in the statutory sense is a live question, and one the regulator has not definitively answered. Entain is, in effect, asking it to answer now. Deal & Reel's earlier coverage of [UK gambling regulation and the white-paper reforms](/uk-gambling-white-paper-analysis) provides useful context on how the current legislative environment took shape.

The precedent, if Entain succeeds, would extend well beyond these three operators. Dozens of Asian-facing bookmakers use European football partnerships as a primary marketing vehicle. A ruling that treats Premier League shirt sponsorships as regulated advertising would force a structural rethink across the sector.

## What This Means for Manila, Kuala Lumpur, and Beyond

For SEA-based operators and investors, the more immediate concern is not what happens in Westminster but what a protracted UK controversy does to the brand equity these sponsorships were bought to generate.

BJ88 and W88 are both active in the Philippines, a market regulated by the Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation (PAGCOR). SBOTOP operates across multiple SEA jurisdictions. In each of these markets, a Premier League badge functions as a credibility signal — shorthand for scale, legitimacy, and the kind of brand investment that local operators cannot easily match. That signal is now under pressure. For background on [PAGCOR's licensing framework and its evolving approach to offshore operators](/pagcor-licensing-framework-overview), see our earlier reporting on the Philippine market.

If UK regulators move against these sponsorships — or if the DCMS consultation produces rules that force clubs to terminate unlicensed partners — the operators lose more than a marketing asset. They lose a reputational anchor that has supported customer acquisition and regulatory goodwill in markets where formal licensing frameworks are still maturing. A club termination, particularly if it is framed publicly as a compliance action, would generate headlines in SEA markets that no amount of local advertising spending could easily offset.

Operators in the region should also note the direction of travel. Regulators in the Philippines and elsewhere have shown increasing interest in aligning their frameworks with international standards. A UK precedent that treats unlicensed Premier League sponsors as operating in a grey zone makes it incrementally easier for SEA regulators to ask the same questions of the same operators at home. ## What to Watch

The DCMS consultation timeline is the critical near-term variable. If the UK government moves to tighten advertising rules before the next Premier League sponsorship cycle — typically negotiated in the spring ahead of the following season — clubs will face pressure to vet partners' regulatory status more rigorously. Watch for any UKGC guidance note on the brand-advertising distinction; that would signal whether Entain's lobbying is gaining traction. For SEA operators with European football partnerships of their own, the window to assess exposure is now, not after a ruling forces the issue.
