# India's OGAI Goes Live, Handing Manila's Prohibitionists a Precedent

India's Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act came into force on May 1, 2026, activating the Online Gaming Authority of India (OGAI) and outlawing every online game played for cash stakes. The regime — a central licensing body, a 90-day classification window, and an outright ban on "online money games" — gives senators in Manila pushing to shut down Philippine iGaming their first large-economy precedent. It also lands an immediate revenue problem on the desks of PAGCOR-licensed operators servicing Indian players, regardless of whether the Philippines itself ever passes a ban.

The PROG Rules 2026 were notified by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) on April 22, 2026, and took effect nine days later, [according to iGaming Business](https://igamingbusiness.com/legal-compliance/regulation/india-publish-new-online-gaming-regulations/). OGAI's registration portal went live the same day.

## What India actually did

The PROG Act draws a single, sharp line. "Online money games" — defined in the statute as any online game in which a user pays money or other valuable consideration with the expectation of monetary or other valuable winnings — are prohibited. That definition captures fantasy sports played for stakes, online poker, online rummy for cash, and any casino-style product. Skill-versus-chance arguments, which kept Indian real-money gaming (RMG) alive through years of state-court litigation, are not a defense under the central law.

E-sports and "permissible social games" — products with no monetary entry and no monetary prize — remain legal and may be registered with OGAI for a 10-year term. The authority sits under MeitY, has 90 days to classify games on application, coordinates with the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND) on anti-money-laundering enforcement, and operates a two-tier grievance mechanism.

The framework replaces a state-by-state grey zone in which Tamil Nadu and Karnataka prohibitions were struck down by their respective high courts, and in which the GST Council's October 2023 decision to tax RMG at 28% on full face value had already triggered industry write-downs the All India Gaming Federation (AIGF) put above $840 million. India's domestic RMG champions — Dream11, My11Circle, PokerBaazi, Adda52, Junglee Rummy, RummyCircle, and A23, among others — now have until OGAI's enforcement clock starts ticking to either pivot to permissible-social-game formats or wind down India-facing operations.

## The Manila ban camp's playbook just got an upgrade

In the Philippines, the locus of the ban debate has been the [Senate Committee on Games and Amusements](#internal-pending), where ban proposals advanced through hearings in the first quarter of 2026. The next major legislative window opens in the second half of the year.

For the prohibitionist camp, India is useful in three specific ways. It is a G20 economy that took the central-legislative route the Philippine Senate is contemplating. It collapsed the skill-versus-chance distinction that PAGCOR-aligned analysts have long used to defend regulated verticals. And it built an institutional regulator — OGAI — rather than relying on state-level enforcement, neutralizing the ["regulation works better than prohibition" rebuttal](#internal-pending) that PAGCOR Chair Alejandro Tengco has deployed in committee.

Expect floor speeches citing India within weeks. The same precedent will likely surface in Indonesia, where online gambling is already prohibited, and in early-stage debates in Vietnam and Thailand, where integrated-resort and online-vertical questions remain unsettled.

## Where the analogy breaks

The comparison is less tidy than its proponents will make it sound. India banned what was almost entirely a domestic-facing industry: Indian-licensed or Indian-operated apps serving Indian residents. The Philippines, by contrast, runs a hybrid model in which a meaningful share of activity under PAGCOR licenses is export-oriented, with offshore-facing operators servicing players outside the country. PAGCOR has publicly cited annualized gross gaming revenue (GGR, the amount wagered minus winnings paid out) contribution above PHP 100 billion from regulated online verticals — a fiscal line item with no direct Indian analogue.

PAGCOR's institutional weight is also different in kind. It is both regulator and, historically, operator; OGAI is a pure regulator built from scratch. And the Philippine framework has been moving toward a [B2B accreditation regime](#internal-pending) rather than away from licensing — the institutional momentum, at the regulator level, runs against prohibition.

None of which means the Senate cannot pass a ban. It means the India template, applied to the Philippines, would be banning a different thing for different reasons.

## The operator exposure that just got real

The more immediate story for Deal & Reel readers is that the Indian revenue line is closing for offshore operators servicing Indian residents — PAGCOR-, Curaçao-, and Anjouan-licensed sites included. OGAI's stated tools include coordination with the Reserve Bank of India and the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India on payment-rail interdiction and geo-blocking, and with app stores on delisting. Operational capacity in month one is untested, but the legal authority is unambiguous.

For PAGCOR-licensed offshore-facing operators, the exposure is concrete and near-term. No PH-licensed operator has publicly disclosed Indian-player revenue concentration or issued guidance on the OGAI rollout; first-quarter filings due May and June will be the first read. Suppliers — aggregators, payment providers, platform vendors — face a parallel decision: whether to exit relationships with delisted Indian-facing operators, and whether to pursue OGAI registration for permissible-social-game products that could absorb some of the displaced demand.

PAGCOR has not, as of publication, issued a formal response to the Indian rollout. Tengco has been asked variants of the precedent question through April; his on-record line has remained that regulation outperforms prohibition.

## The Takeaway

OGAI is a policy artifact with immediate operational consequences. The Philippine ban camp now has rhetorical ammunition it did not have a week ago, but rhetorical ammunition is not a template — the two markets are structured too differently for the Indian playbook to port cleanly. What does port, immediately, is the revenue hit to offshore operators with Indian player exposure. Watch the Q1 filings, watch the Senate hearing schedule for the second half of 2026, and watch whether OGAI's enforcement capacity in months two and three matches the statute's reach. The legislative debate in Manila will run on its own clock; the operator damage is already running on India's.
