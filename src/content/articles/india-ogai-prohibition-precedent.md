The Office of Gaming Affairs India (OGAI) officially commenced operations on May 1, marking the subcontinent's first dedicated federal regulator for online gaming and betting — and sending ripples across Southeast Asia's own fractious regulatory landscape.

## A New Regulatory Benchmark

New Delhi's decision to centralise oversight under a single body mirrors the trajectory seen in Europe a decade earlier, where fragmented state-level licensing gave way to national frameworks in countries like Germany (GlüStV 2021) and the Netherlands (KSA regime). OGAI's mandate covers licensing, responsible gambling enforcement, advertising standards, and tax collection — a comprehensive scope that contrasts sharply with the patchwork approach still common across Asia-Pacific.

The immediate practical impact is a licensing window. Operators with Indian-facing revenues above ₹50 crore ($5.9 million) must apply for an OGAI licence within 180 days or face blocking orders enforced via ISP and payment-processor cooperation. Sources close to the regulator say **at least 40 applications** are already in the pipeline, including from subsidiaries of Flutter, Entain, and several prominent domestic fantasy-sports platforms pivoting into real-money gaming.

## Why Manila Is Watching

The timing is no coincidence. In the Philippines, the push to ban all offshore gaming operators (POGOs) gained fresh legislative momentum in Q1 2026 after a Senate inquiry linked several POGO licensees to organised crime and human trafficking. PAGCOR's own roadmap envisions a "post-POGO" era in which domestically licensed online operators serve Philippine residents through a new iGaming framework tentatively called *eGames 2.0*.

India's OGAI launch hands Manila's prohibitionist caucus a powerful talking point: **a large, comparable Asian market has chosen strict federal regulation over prohibition** — and is already attracting major international operators willing to pay for legitimacy. Filipino senators on the fence can now point to New Delhi and argue that licensing, not blanket bans, is the modern consensus.

## Revenue Projections and Tax Design

OGAI's tax model is a 28% GST on gross gaming revenue (GGR), a rate that drew criticism from operators during the consultation period but ultimately survived intact. The Ministry of Finance projects ₹12,000 crore ($1.4 billion) in annual tax revenue by FY2028 — a figure industry analysts consider optimistic but not outlandish given India's 500-million-plus smartphone user base and a legal real-money gaming market that consultancy KPMG estimated at $2.8 billion in 2025.

The tax design also includes a 1% TDS (tax deducted at source) on net player winnings above ₹10,000 per transaction, a mechanism intended to create a paper trail for anti-money-laundering compliance.

## Takeaway

India's regulatory leap is the most significant structural change in Asian iGaming since Macau's concession re-tender in 2022. For operators, it opens a vast addressable market under (relatively) clear rules. For regulators elsewhere in the region — particularly in the Philippines, Vietnam, and Thailand — it raises the political cost of inaction. The question is no longer *whether* Asia will regulate online gaming, but *how fast* the remaining holdouts will follow New Delhi's lead.
