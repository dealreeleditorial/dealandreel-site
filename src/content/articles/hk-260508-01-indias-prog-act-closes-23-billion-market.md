# India's PROG Act Closes a $2–3 Billion Market — Southeast Asia Is Watching

India's Online Gaming (Prevention and Regulation) Act 2026 (PROG Act) came into force on 1 May 2026, formally hardening the country's ban on real-money iGaming and establishing the Online Gaming Authority of India (OGAI) as the central enforcement body. For operators and investors tracking Asia, the question is not what India has closed — it is where the displaced capital and demand will land.

The answer is not straightforward. Southeast Asia's regulatory landscape is fragmented, and the opportunity created by India's exit is real but unevenly distributed across jurisdictions.

## What the PROG Act Actually Does

The legislation does two things simultaneously. It creates a formal licensing framework for permissible "skill game" categories — a carve-out that OGAI will administer — while categorically excluding real-money wagering from that framework. The effect is a hard ceiling on any licensed iGaming activity in a market that H2 Gambling Capital and Mordor Intelligence have separately sized at between $2 billion and $3 billion in gross gaming revenue (GGR, the amount wagered minus winnings paid out).

That figure requires context. India's online gaming market operated largely in regulatory grey space before the PROG Act. Enforcement of the pre-existing ban was inconsistent; offshore platforms served Indian-origin players through payment workarounds and, increasingly, VPNs. The PROG Act does not eliminate that grey market overnight. OGAI has not yet published enforcement timelines or transitional provisions for existing operators, [according to iGaming Business reporting from 1 May 2026](https://www.igamingbusiness.com/). What the Act does is remove any remaining ambiguity about the legal pathway — there is none for real-money gaming.

For legitimate operators who had been watching India as a medium-term licensing opportunity, that ambiguity is now resolved. The question becomes where to redeploy attention and capital.

## The Philippine Position

The Philippines is the most structurally prepared to absorb redirected operator interest. [PAGCOR](https://www.pagcor.ph/) (Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation), the country's primary gaming regulator, has an established licensing framework for offshore online operators — historically structured as POGOs (Philippine Offshore Gaming Operators) and, under a rebranded framework, IGOs (Internet Gaming Operators). The Philippines offers a known regulatory pathway, an English-language legal environment, and a regulator with a track record of processing international operator applications.

PAGCOR's current licensing pipeline and active operator count data [verify — PAGCOR.ph figures not confirmed in brief] would indicate the depth of remaining capacity. What is clear from the regulatory record is that the Philippines has survived significant political pressure on offshore gaming — including a partial POGO suspension in prior years — and has maintained a functional licensing infrastructure. For operators seeking a legitimate Asian base, that institutional continuity matters.

The caveat is that Philippine-licensed operators cannot legally serve Indian residents. The arbitrage play here is not direct market access — it is operator positioning: companies that pivot regional infrastructure to the Philippines can serve other Asian markets from a licensed base while India remains closed.

## The Broader SEA Picture

Cambodia and other SEA jurisdictions present a different calculus. Cambodia's licensing framework, administered through the General Department of Casino Regulation, has historically attracted operators seeking lower barriers to entry. That permissiveness cuts both ways: faster licensing timelines, but less regulatory credibility with international banking partners and payment processors. For operators prioritising speed over institutional standing, Cambodia remains an option. For those seeking investor confidence, the Philippines holds the stronger hand.

Malaysia, Indonesia, and Vietnam are not credible near-term alternatives. All three maintain effective prohibitions on licensed online gaming for domestic players, and none has signalled regulatory liberalisation. Singapore's framework is tightly controlled and oriented toward its two integrated resort licensees — not a destination for new offshore entrants.

The realistic SEA beneficiary list is short: the Philippines first, Cambodia as a secondary option for specific operator profiles, and a handful of smaller jurisdictions — Isle of Man-licensed operators with Asian operations, for instance — that may use India's closure as a marketing moment with Asian investors.

## Capital Flows, Not Just Operators

The more immediate market signal may come from investment rather than operations. Private equity and venture funds tracking Asian gaming have been underweight the sector since India's regulatory direction became apparent through the 2023–2024 Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) framework consultations. With the PROG Act now in force, that wait-and-see posture resolves into a binary: redeploy toward accessible markets or exit Asian gaming exposure.

PAGCOR-licensed platforms and Cambodia-based operators with clean compliance records are the most likely recipients of redirected institutional capital. Suppliers — platform providers, payment processors, KYC vendors — serving those operators stand to benefit from any volume uptick.

## What to Watch

OGAI's enforcement posture in the coming months will determine how much of India's grey-market GGR migrates versus simply contracting. If OGAI moves aggressively on payment blocking and VPN enforcement, operator pivots toward SEA-licensed platforms will accelerate. Watch also for any India-origin gaming companies filing for PAGCOR or Cambodian licences — the first such application will be a concrete signal that the market has accepted India's closure as permanent. Investors should track PAGCOR's licensing pipeline data as a leading indicator of regional capital reallocation.
